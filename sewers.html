Managing Stimulation in an ADHD affliced mind

September 7, 2026

Today I pin-pointed another bad habit of mine. I’m not entirely sure if it’s yet another implication of ADHD as I’ve seen far too many a man entertain this habit to label them all ADHD victims. Here’s the habit: When I feel that I’ve been productive (ex. Completed a lot of tasks or overcame a big obstacle), after the matter, I seek out an equally stimulating reward, however destructive it may be. Why? Perhaps because I was building up the expectation of being stimulated after the completion of the productive day/task. Likewise, when I’ve had an unproductive day, or monotonously uneventful day, I don’t seek any gratifying stimulation at the end of the day. Because I wasn’t expecting any, so I’m perfectly content going on without it. Ironically, by the end of days (i.e. before I go to bed) I always feel generally happier and more content on days in which I did nothing because I had no expectations. Also, given how excessive stimulation can worsen ADHD symptoms it’s no wonder why what I mentioned above is the case.

What I think is that in my ADHD afflicted brain, the stimulation my brain receives and demands has a snowballing effect. So, the more I supply it, the more it demands (this is the case for normal people too, but for me the snowball amasses much quicker). This makes sense as in the hours before I go to bed my stimulation expectation seems to be at its peak (the snowball has been growing throughout the day). Sleeping does seem to be a reset though. So basic logic would have me believe that in order to feel content I need to do things which bring me only trace amounts of stimulation, or none at all. (brutal)

Ok, but sometimes I want to do tasks that give me great amounts of stimulation – though I try to limit them in number. For example, updating my site (which I’m not willing to give up); so how can I cope with this?

Is the solution to expect no external stimulation once the task has been successfully carried out? To look for and contain the feeling of stimulation within the task itself? This sounds like it could work. I should give myself time after a completed task to slow down and feel happy about it before jumping to the next task to allow my brain to reset its baseline stimulation expectation (reset the tumbling snowball so-to-speak).

To summarize, I should be aiming to keep the rolling snowball as little and possible throughout the day, and if I do choose to experience a large dose of stimulation, I should employ the coping mechanism outlined in the paragraph above.
